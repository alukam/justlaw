The Fallacy of AI Confidentiality and Practical Safeguards

The rapid integration of generative artificial intelligence into pre-litigation research and case strategy has created a dangerous illusion that queries including documents uploaded into an AI model are as protected as a confidential consultation with a licensed attorney.

In reality, uploading factual claims, litigation theories, or client secrets into commercial AI tools routinely exposes that information to disclosure and waives core evidentiary protections.

This chapter examines why information uploaded to AI platforms fails to meet the legal standards for privilege and outlines concrete, enforceable protocols to safeguard sensitive legal work product.

Evidentiary privileges, specifically the attorney-client privilege and the work product doctrine, are strictly construed exceptions to the general rule that the public is entitled to every person's evidence, as illustrated in In re Grand Jury Subpoenas.

In In re Grand Jury Subpoenas, the government argued that the work product doctrine applies only to materials prepared by or at the direction of counsel in anticipation of litigation.

When a party inputs case facts or legal theories into a commercial generative AI model, the interaction may fail to satisfy the elements required to establish privilege.

The attorney-client privilege generally requires a communication:

  1. Between a client and an attorney;
  2. Intended to be, and kept, confidential;
  3. Made for the primary purpose of obtaining or providing legal advice.

Commercial AI platforms may raise concerns regarding these requirements, particularly because they are not attorneys and may involve third-party systems.

In the case of United States v. Heppner, Bradley Heppner, CEO of Beneficient, was indicted for securities fraud, wire fraud, and related charges involving a scheme that allegedly misappropriated more than $300 million.

Prior to his arrest, Heppner independently ran approximately 31 prompts through Anthropic's public AI model, Claude.

His prompts incorporated details of the government's investigation, including strategic legal insights he had learned from his counsel.

Heppner generated these documents for the stated purpose of analyzing his exposure and discussing the outputs with his defense team.

Defense counsel did not direct or supervise these searches. Federal agents later seized electronic devices containing the AI-generated documents during the execution of a search warrant.

Defense counsel asserted privilege over the files, leading to a dispute regarding whether privilege attached to the AI-generated material.
